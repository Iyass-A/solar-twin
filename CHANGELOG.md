# Changelog

## Unreleased

- Trip planner, part 1: **the Hohmann transfer** (experimental). A new card under "branch reality", in all three languages: pick a planet and it shows how long the trip from Earth takes, the burn to leave a 300 km orbit round Earth (with the launch energy C3), the burn to stop in a 300 km orbit round the planet (with the speed it arrives at), both together, where the planet must be at launch (ahead of or behind Earth, as seen from the Sun) and where it is at the time on screen, the launch window next to that time and the arrival date, and how often windows come (the synodic period). A button goes to the launch date and picks the planet. For Mars: 259 days, 3.59 + 2.09 = 5.68 km/s, C3 8.67 km²/s², Mars 44.3° ahead, next on 4 December 2026 (arriving 19 August 2027). Both orbits are taken as circles in one plane, so the card says it is an estimate; for the outer planets it adds that real spacecraft use gravity assists and stretched orbits. `npm run check` reproduces Curtis' worked example (Earth to Mars), flies every trip with the universal-variable propagator (it arrives at the target's orbit with the right speed, and so does the target), compares the synodic periods with NASA's fact sheets, and finds every launch window from 1800 to 2050 (2,036, one per synodic period); breaking the phase angle, the escape term, the window search, the synodic period or the transfer time on purpose was caught.
- Trip planner, part 2: **Lambert's problem** (`src/transfer/lambert.ts`): the path round the Sun from Earth on one date to a planet on another, on the real, stretched and tilted orbits (universal variables with bisection, as in Vallado and Curtis), from the planets' exact positions and velocities (Astronomy Engine). Under the Hohmann estimate the card now has two date fields, starting at that estimate's dates: for any two days it gives the time on the way, both burns with the launch energy C3 and the arrival speed, both together, and how far round the Sun the path goes (type I, less than half a turn, or type II, more). For Mars, the Hohmann dates (4 December 2026 to 20 August 2027) need a C3 of 19.8 km²/s²; NASA's best 2026 trajectory (31 October 2026 to 19 August 2027) needs 9.18. `npm run check` reproduces Curtis' example 5.2 and Vallado's 7-5, finds again 397 orbits flown first with the propagator (short and long way, hyperbolas), checks the planets' states against the site's planets and their own motion, and compares the three best 2026 Mars trajectories in NASA's mission design handbook (NASA/TM-2010-216764): C3 within 0.7%, arrival v∞ within 0.001 km/s, launch declination within 0.15°, same types; breaking the long way, the sign of A, the arrival velocity, the velocity units, the departure v∞, the declination's frame or the bisection on purpose was caught each time.
- Trip planner, part 3: **a porkchop map of launch dates** (`src/transfer/porkchop.ts`, `src/view/porkchopMap.ts`). Between the Hohmann estimate and the exact trip, a map of every launch day (across) and time on the way (up) round the window, each trip worked out exactly and coloured in bands of one blue by launch energy C3 or by both burns (Δv), lighter for cheaper, with a legend, the cheapest trip of each type (I and II) marked and listed with a button that works it out below, the Hohmann estimate marked H, a dashed line between the types, and the trip worked out below as a white ring. Hover or tap shows a tooltip; a click, a tap or the arrow keys (Shift for ten days) pick a trip. The map is worked out in slices of a few milliseconds once the card comes into view (about half a second), and the exact trip then moves to the cheapest one unless dates were already picked. A cheapest trip on the map's edge says so (Neptune's type II, at 49 years). The best trips are searched outside 1° of half a turn, where the plane of the path is barely defined: in late 2026 Mars arrives almost on the ecliptic and a search there lands on a numerical spike. Lambert's solver now uses the Illinois method (twice as fast, same answers). `npm run check` compares the cheapest launch energy of every Mars opportunity from 2026 to 2045 with NASA's handbook (table 1: all within 0.9%, the same type, not on the map's edge or the ridge, a true valley bottom), and checks that a map worked out in slices is the one worked out at once, that each cell is the trip worked out on its own (Mars and Neptune), the types, the band edges and the texts; breaking the arrival dates, the ridge, the measures, the refinement, the bands or the time step on purpose was caught each time.
- Trip planner, part 4: **the trip in the views**. Under the exact trip, "Play the trip in the view" sets the time to its launch day and runs it at the speed that takes closest to 15 seconds (a month per second for Mars), in the inner view for Mercury, Venus and Mars, picks the planet and scrolls up to the view; time stops on arrival day. In 3D and on the map the trip's path round the Sun is drawn from its start (the universal-variable propagator), solid behind the spacecraft and dashed ahead, with a ring in Earth's colour where it leaves and one in the planet's where it arrives, and the spacecraft as a small white diamond with its name. A checkbox shows or hides the path. `npm run check` checks that six trips' paths (Mercury to Neptune) start at the Earth and end at their target within a metre, and the run speeds; breaking the speed choice or the trip's starting velocity on purpose was caught.
- Trip planner, part 5: **the signal delay** (`src/transfer/signal.ts`, `src/view/signalChart.ts`). Under the exact trip, a line chart of how long a radio signal takes one way between Earth and the spacecraft on each day of the trip (its distance divided by the speed of light), with the longest delay and the one at arrival labelled, the time on screen as a line, a tooltip by mouse, touch or arrow keys (Shift for ten steps), and the numbers as a table. Light bands (or, past eight of them, marks along the top) show the days when the spacecraft is within 2° of the Sun in Earth's sky, when NASA's Mars missions send no commands because the corona garbles the signal; the card lists them (the first and the last when there are many). Lines give the delay at arrival with a question and its answer (twice that), the longest delay and its date, and the delay at the time on screen. While a trip plays, the spacecraft's label in the views carries its delay. For NASA's best 2026 Mars trip: seconds after launch, 15.7 minutes on arrival (31.3 there and back), never near the Sun; to Jupiter (December 2027 to July 2031): up to 53.9 minutes, three times near the Sun. `npm run check` compares the delay and the Sun's angle at Mars every 5 days for a year with JPL Horizons (within 0.007 s and 0.001°), finds Mars within 2° of the Sun on the days of NASA's pauses (28 August to 7 September 2019, 2 to 16 October 2021, 11 to 25 November 2023), checks that the distance at one instant stays within 1.3×10⁻⁴ of the true light time, and checks the longest delay and every stretch near the Sun of six trips against a plain scan; breaking the Sun's angle, the speed of light, the search between samples, the edges of a stretch, the search for the longest delay or the distance on purpose was caught each time.

## v0.5.0 (15 November 2026), public beta

- Version 0.5.0. **Branch reality** with our own physics engine: change the solar system (take a planet out, change a mass, push a planet, let a star pass) and watch the copy run next to reality, with a table of the differences, or start one of three ready-made scenarios with one click. The tagline and link preview mention it, with a new preview image (the Sun twice as heavy: empty circles show where the planets would be).
- Physics engine, part 1: **the starting state.** Position and velocity of the Sun, the eight planets, the seven moons larger than Pluto (Moon, Io, Europa, Ganymede, Callisto, Titan, Triton) and the barycentres of the nine planetary systems (Pluto's too) at 15 November 2026, 00:00 TDB, from JPL Horizons, with their masses from DE440 and JPL's satellite table (`src/physics/`, `src/data/state-vectors.json`). A planet stands for itself and its smaller moons; each planet and its moons are moved together onto their system's barycentre (up to 1,200 km, for Uranus), keeping the moons' orbits. A new workflow (`fetch-state-vectors.yml`, by hand only, read-only) fetches them and positions every 5 days for a year after, to check the engine against later. Nothing on the page changes yet. `npm run check` checks the masses against known ratios and Horizons' own, the states against Astronomy Engine and the site's planets, and that every moon is bound to its planet at its known distance, going the known way round (Triton backwards).
- Physics engine, part 2: **the integrator** (`src/physics/nbody.ts`): Newtonian gravity between all bodies, stepped with the leapfrog (velocity Verlet) or Yoshida's 4th-order composition of it, both symplectic and time-reversible, plus the equatorial bulges (J2) of Jupiter, Saturn and Neptune, without which Io would be some 90° off after a year. Against Horizons after a year: the planets within 0.001° (Mercury, 1,200 km, before relativity was added in part 4), the moons within 0.22° round their planet (Titan), the energy within 3·10⁻¹⁰, and a year takes about 20 ms as systems, 0.25 s with the moons. `npm run check` tests the order of each method on a Kepler orbit, running backwards to the start, momentum, angular momentum and energy (also over a century), the bulges (each moon near its planet's equator, Triton backwards), and the year against Horizons.
- Physics engine, part 3: **a Web Worker** (`src/physics/worker.ts`), so the page never waits for it. The page names its simulations, starts them as systems or with moons, branches one off another, and asks for a time (`src/physics/client.ts`); the worker runs towards the latest time asked in slices of 8 ms, so a newer request (the time bar moved again) replaces an older one at once, and long runs report progress. The results are exactly those of stepping directly, in Chrome as in Node. In Chrome: a year as systems in about 10 ms, 200 years in 0.35 s, with the page never pausing more than 12 ms. `npm run check` runs the same engine: same numbers, a replaced run, branches, and refusals. Nothing on the page uses it yet.
- Physics engine, part 4: **checked over the whole time bar.** General relativity near the Sun (first post-Newtonian order, as REBOUNDx "gr"): it turns Mercury's perihelion by 42.91″ a century (known: 42.98″) and takes Mercury from 1,162 km to 47 km off Horizons after a year. Because it depends on velocity, the last half kick of each step is solved for the new velocity; without that the orbits slowly lost energy (Mercury 2° off after a century). The default step is now 1/400 of the shortest orbit at its closest point, from each orbit's size and eccentricity, so it no longer depends on where the planets are at the start (0.156 day as systems, 0.0044 day with moons). Back to 1800 and on to 2050 against Astronomy Engine: every planet within 0.017° (Mercury, 1820), a tenth of the site's Kepler planets (0.21°); "good enough to show" is set at 0.05°. In Chrome, 200 years take 1.4 s while the page never pauses more than 11 ms. `npm run check` adds the relativity test and the whole time bar; breaking the relativity sign, the solved kick, the step or the eccentricity term on purpose was caught each time.
- Physics engine, part 5a: **changes for "branch reality"** (`edit` in `src/physics/protocol.ts`, `client.edit()`): take a body out (a planet with its moons), multiply its mass, push it faster or slower along its motion (its moons along), or add one, such as a passing star, at the sim's current time. The step and the energy baseline follow the changed system. `npm run check` tests each against physics: without Jupiter the momentum drops by exactly Jupiter's; with the Sun twice as heavy Earth swings between 0.334 and 0.989 au, as vis-viva says; Earth pushed by 5 km/s goes out to 2.174 au (vis-viva: 2.173); a star aimed to pass at 1,000 au in 100 years passes at 999.9 au after 100.1 years. Through the worker's engine the same changes give exactly the same numbers; bad changes are refused. Breaking the bookkeeping on purpose (bulges or the Sun's index after a removal, moons left behind by a push, a point added from the wrong origin, an old energy baseline) was caught each time. Nothing on the page uses it yet.
- **Branch reality** (part 5b, experimental): a new card under the time controls. Pick what to change (take a planet out with its moons, multiply the Sun's or a planet's mass, push a planet faster or slower along its motion, or let a star pass, by default 50 au away in 10 years at 20 km/s in the plane of Earth's orbit) and press "Branch": the engine copies the system at the date on screen, changes it and runs the copy on as the time bar moves. On the map and in 3D the copy's planets are empty circles with their recent path (positions along each run come with its result, so the path follows the curve even at a year per second), and a dotted line to the real planet once they drift apart; a star off the map gets an arrow at the edge with its distance in au. Before the branch's date the card says the branch does not exist yet; "Back to reality" removes it. The engine starts only when the card is first used (its file is 14 kB). Forking takes under a second in Chrome. A star must pass before 2050, so it can be watched. In all three languages. `npm run check` also checks the positions along a run.
- **Branch reality, compared** (part 6): the engine's reality now runs on next to the branch, the same calculation without the change, and the card compares them at the same moment for each planet (and a passing star): its year on its orbit now, worked out from its position and velocity relative to the Sun ("escaping" when it no longer orbits), its distance from the Sun, and how far it is from where it would be (km, million km or au), with the real value under each. So every difference shown comes from the change alone: a year after the Sun doubles, Earth's year is 139 days; a year without Jupiter, Mars is 106,000 km from its real place; with a star passing 1,000 au away, Neptune is 2,300 km off by 2030. "Go to the start of the branch" brings the time back. In 3D, a passing star off the screen gets an arrow at the edge with its distance, as on the map. The worker now sends velocities with each state and masses with each "ready". `npm run check` checks the years from the orbits now (Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Neptune within 0.02%), that Earth 13 km/s faster escapes, that Earth's new year with the Sun doubled is the one timed from closest point to closest point (139.0 against 139.1 days), and that velocities and masses through the worker are exactly those of doing it directly; breaking the year or eccentricity formula on purpose was caught.
- **Ready-made scenarios** (part 7): three buttons above the branch form, in all three languages. Each fills in the form (so it can be changed from there), goes to its date, branches and runs time at a fitting speed, and says what it does and what comes out: **What if Jupiter vanished?** (from 1 January 1900, a year per second: nothing flies off or falls into the Sun, but the orbits shift; by 2000 Saturn is about 220 million km from its real place and Earth about 700,000 km), **What if the Sun were twice as heavy?** (from 15 November 2026, a month per second: Earth's year becomes 139 days, it comes within 0.33 au of the Sun, and Jupiter's year is 4.7 years), and **A star passes 1,000 au away** (a star like the Sun in 2036, a year per second: nothing shows by eye; by the end of 2050 Neptune is only about 70,000 km off and Earth less than 100 km), with a button to bring the same star in to 50 au (Neptune's year about 100 instead of 165, Uranus's about 110 instead of 84, Neptune about 400 million km off). Links: `#branch-jupiter`, `#branch-sun`, `#branch-star`, `#branch-star50`; the address follows the scenario on show. `npm run check` runs each scenario through the engine as the page does and checks every number its text gives (and that every language gives them); moving a fact, a start date or the star's direction on purpose was caught.
- The passing star now comes closest at ecliptic longitude −30° instead of 0°, so its arrow at the edge of the views no longer covers the equinox label. Its distance there has a thousands separator.
- Fixes in "branch reality": the copy's planets are drawn at the time on screen (on their orbit round the Sun, from the engine's last answer, which is a few frames old: at a year per second Mercury's circle used to trail its planet by tens of degrees); a branch made while time runs now starts at the date asked (a late answer for the time bar's previous request could make it start where reality was, years earlier); and progress from a branch that was replaced no longer shows for the new one. While a branch is being prepared, the card says so, with the year reality has reached.

## v0.4.0 (15 November 2026), public beta

- Live data, part 1: **close approaches to Earth.** A new card lists every asteroid and comet passing closer than 0.05 au (7.5 million km, 19.5 lunar distances) from a week ago to two months ahead, from NASA/JPL's CNEOS (SBDB Close-Approach Data API): date and time in the visitor's time zone, how long until or since, distance in lunar distances and km (with the 3σ range when it is wide, and the 3σ time uncertainty when it is half an hour or more), speed, and the measured size or one estimated from the brightness. A summary gives the count, how many come closer than the Moon, and the closest; the last week can be shown too, with a line for now. Bodies that are on the map can be picked from the list. In all three languages.
- Live data, part 2: **space weather.** A second card shows NOAA's space weather scales now and their highest in the last 24 hours (radio blackouts R, solar radiation storms S, geomagnetic storms G, each with what it does), the forecast for the next days, the 3-hour Kp index of the last 3 days and its forecast as a chart (colour by storm level with a legend, a line for now, lighter forecast bars, a tooltip by mouse, touch or arrow keys, and the values as a table), the solar flares of the last 7 days with the strongest, and the CMEs that NASA's WSA-Enlil runs (DONKI) expect to reach Earth, marked as experimental. Each part says when it is missing or more than 12 hours old.
- Live data, part 3: **Deep Space Network now.** A third card shows which spacecraft NASA's antennas at Goldstone, Madrid and Canberra are talking to (NASA's DSN Now): how many are in contact and the farthest of them, and for each site its local time and its antennas (size, elevation, what they are doing), with each spacecraft's data rate down (↓), whether we send to it (↑), its distance, the time its signal takes, and roughly where it is (orbiting Mars, on its way to Jupiter, in interstellar space…). Spacecraft names in all three languages for the well-known missions, NASA's names for the rest. The page asks once a minute, and not while the tab is hidden.
- Because this data changes every few seconds, it comes through a small server of our own on Cloudflare Workers (free plan, `workers/dsn/`) instead of a file built every 3 hours: it asks NASA at most once a minute, turns the XML into a small JSON, gives it only to pages on our site (CORS), sets no cookies and logs nothing. A new workflow (`deploy-worker.yml`) publishes it when the Cloudflare secrets exist (otherwise it does nothing), then tests it as our page and as another site would. It runs at `https://solar-twin-dsn.solar-twin.workers.dev/`; pages that are not our published site (such as `npm run dev`) do not ask it, and the card says there is no live data there. `npm run check` tests the XML reader and parser on real files from NASA, the refusals, the server's rules (only our site, NASA at most once a minute, an old copy for up to 10 minutes when NASA fails) and the new texts.
- **Now in space:** the three live cards sit under one screen with a tile for each (Deep Space Network, space weather, close approaches): its headline (spacecraft in contact and the farthest; storm levels now, the highest NOAA expects and a CME on its way; approaches coming and the closest) and when it was last updated. A tile opens its card, so only one card shows at a time; arrow keys move between tiles. `#now`, `#now-dsn`, `#now-weather` and `#now-approaches` open the screen or a card from a link. The "Now" button of the time controls has the id `nowBtn` now, so `#now` means the screen.
- The live data is fetched on GitHub before each deploy and every 3 hours, checked (`src/live/`) and published with the site; nothing is committed and the workflow needs no new permissions. If a source does not answer, the copy on the site stays for that part, and the cards say when data is old. Both shapes of SWPC's Kp JSON (changed in 2026) are read. `npm run check` tests the parsers, refusals of changed formats, sizes, times, storm levels, the grouping of model runs by CME and the new texts, and checks the live files where they exist.

## v0.3.0 (15 November 2026), public beta

- Licensed under the GNU AGPL v3.0 or later (`LICENSE`). Copyright (C) 2026 Iyass-A.
- 2,092 asteroids, dwarf planets and comets from JPL, in the 3D view and on the map, as coloured dots by group (dwarf planet, asteroid, near-Earth asteroid, comet): every near-Earth asteroid of about 1 km and larger, the 400 brightest numbered asteroids of the main belt and Jupiter trojans, the 300 brightest bodies beyond Neptune, all numbered periodic comets, and 36 well-known ones with names in all three languages and a table of their own. The others use JPL's names; a search box finds any of them by name or number, and the legend counts each group. Pick one to see its orbit, its type, size and distances, and to follow it with the camera. A switch hides them.
- Each of them shows only in the years where our Sun-only orbit stays within 1° of JPL Horizons (sampled every 5 years over 25 years each way); outside, the info panel says why.
- The data comes from a GitHub workflow: current orbits for each group in one request (SBDB Query API), each orbit's covariance (SBDB API, at most 80 requests a minute, as JPL blocks faster clients for a minute), and Horizons positions and uncertainties for the checks. `npm run check` compares every body with Horizons.
- Uncertainty clouds: around a picked asteroid or comet, 300 possible orbits drawn from JPL's covariance show where it could be, enlarged with a label when too small to see; the info panel gives the real size (1σ, in m or km). 1,690 bodies get one: those fitted with gravity alone whose cloud is within 0.8 to 1.25 times Horizons' own uncertainty, which `npm run check` confirms for every one. Their covariances sit in 16 small files, loaded only when a body is picked. Other bodies get an explanation instead of a cloud.
- The less-known dots are a little smaller and dimmer than the planets and the 36 well-known bodies; a tap near a planet picks the planet.
- Fixes: "2024 YR4" and comet designations keep their order inside Arabic text; very precise orbits (Bennu) no longer lose their perihelion-time uncertainty to rounding.
- The page links to its source code, and the licence notices of three.js, Astronomy Engine and the star data are published with it (`THIRD-PARTY-LICENSES.txt`).
